\section{Integration schemes\label{sec:Integration-schemes}}

In order to integrate the homogeneous differential equations of motion
we discretize time as $t_{n}=n\Delta t$ with $n=1,\ldots,N$, where
$\Delta t$ is the timestep (\texttt{timestep}) and $N=\left\lceil T/\Delta t\right\rceil $
is the number of steps, $T$ being the \texttt{final time}. A ratio
$T/\Delta t$ within a relative $10^{-12}$ of an integer counts as that
integer, so that every compiler makes the same number of steps (at
least one). The last step is a full $\Delta t$: a final time that is
not a multiple of $\Delta t$ is overshot to $N\Delta t$. JETSPIN offers
four integration schemes, selected by the directive \texttt{integrator}
as described in Sec \ref{sec:Input-file}: the first-order accurate Euler
scheme (\texttt{integrator 1}), the second-order accurate Heun scheme,
sometimes called second-order accurate Runge-Kutta (\texttt{integrator 2}),
the classical fourth-order accurate Runge-Kutta scheme\cite{press2007numerical}
(\texttt{integrator 3}), and the stochastic scheme by Platen described
below (\texttt{integrator 4}). The Kelvin\textendash Voigt model
(\texttt{kvfluid yes}), with or without evaporation, supports only the
integrators 1\textendash 3. The Maxwell model, with or without evaporation,
supports all four, the Platen scheme with \texttt{system 4} only: with
\texttt{system 1} or \texttt{3} it stops the run at the first step
(message ``ERROR - ftype is wrong!''). The range of \texttt{integrator}
is not checked when the input file is read: a value outside 1\textendash 4,
or \texttt{integrator 4} with the Kelvin\textendash Voigt model, stops
the run at the first step (message ``ERROR - ktype error.'').

The stochastic terms of the three dimensional model, namely the random
airdrag force (\texttt{airdrag amplitude}) and the
velocity noise (\texttt{noise yes}), require \texttt{system 4} (warnings
35 and 95) and the explicit strong order scheme by Platen
\cite{platen1987derivative,kloeden1992numerical,platen2010numerical}
(\texttt{integrator 4}, warnings 34 and 94), whereof the order of strong
convergence was evaluated equal to 1.5; in JETSPIN this order refers to
the update of the velocities (see below). With \texttt{airdrag yes},
\texttt{system 4} and \texttt{integrator 4}, the directive \texttt{airdrag
amplitude} is mandatory (warning 33). The deterministic airdrag force
alone can be integrated by any of the schemes; the one dimensional model
(\texttt{system 1}) and the Kelvin\textendash Voigt model have no airdrag
term.

The Platen scheme avoids the use of derivatives by corresponding finite
differences in the same way as Runge-Kutta schemes do for ODEs in
a deterministic setting, and it is briefly summarized as follows.

Let us consider a Brownian motion vector process $\textbf{X}=\left\{ \textbf{X}_{t},t\right\} $
of \textit{d}-dimensional satisfying the stochastic differential equation

\begin{equation}
d\textbf{X}=\textbf{a}\left(t,X^{1},\ldots,X^{d}\right)dt+\textbf{b}\,d\Omega\label{eq:generic-sde}
\end{equation}
where $\textbf{a}$ and $\textbf{b}$ are vectors of \textit{d}-dimensional
usually called drift and diffusion vector coefficients, and $\Omega\left(t\right)$
denotes a Wiener process. Denoted $Y_{t}^{k}$ the approximation for
the \textit{k}-th component of the vector $\textbf{X}$ at time $t$,
the integrator has the following form:

\begin{equation}
Y_{t+\varDelta t}^{k}=Y_{t}^{k}+b^{k}\Delta\Omega+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\Delta t}}\left[a^{k}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{+}\right)-a^{k}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{-}\right)\right]\Delta\varPsi+\frac{1}{4}\left[a^{k}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{+}\right)+2a^{k}+a^{k}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{-}\right)\right]\Delta t,\label{eq:integrator-platen}
\end{equation}

with the vector supporting values

\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{\pm}=\textbf{Y}_{t}+\textbf{a}\Delta t\pm\textbf{b}\sqrt{\Delta t}.
\end{equation}
The general scheme contains further terms, with the differences of
$\textbf{b}$ at the supporting values $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{\pm}$
and $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\pm}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{+}\pm\textbf{b}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{+}\right)\sqrt{\Delta t}$,
which vanish for an additive noise. They are not needed in JETSPIN,
whose diffusion coefficient does not depend on the velocities (it
depends on the state only through the bead mass, which changes only
by evaporation): $\textbf{b}$ is evaluated at the beginning of the step.

Here, $\Delta\Omega$ and $\Delta\varPsi$ indicate normally distributed
random variables constructed from two independent $N\left(0,1\right)$
standard Gaussian distributed random variables ($U_{1}$, $U_{2}$)
by means of the following linear transformation:

\begin{equation}
\begin{alignedat}{1}\Delta\Omega & =U_{1}\sqrt{\Delta t}\\
\Delta\varPsi & =\frac{1}{2}\Delta t^{3/2}\left(U_{1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}U_{2}\right).
\end{alignedat}
\label{eq:random-var-1}
\end{equation}

JETSPIN applies Eq \ref{eq:integrator-platen} to the velocities only,
the components that carry the noise; in the supporting values
$\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{\pm}$ the positions and the stresses
are advanced by an Euler step, without noise. The positions are then
advanced by a Heun step,
\begin{equation}
\textbf{x}_{n+1}=\textbf{x}_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\left(\textbf{f}_{1}+\textbf{f}_{2}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\textbf{f}_{1}$ is the derivative at the beginning of the step
(the velocity $\textbf{v}_{n}$) and $\textbf{f}_{2}$ is evaluated at
the Euler-predicted positions $\textbf{x}_{n}+\Delta t\,\textbf{f}_{1}$
and at the new velocities $\textbf{v}_{n+1}$. The stresses are advanced
by a Heun step as well, with $\textbf{f}_{2}$ evaluated at the new positions
and velocities and at the Euler-predicted stresses. With solvent evaporation
(Sec \ref{sec:Evaporation}) the volumes of the beads follow the same
Heun step as the positions.

Note that JETSPIN integrates the equations in the dimensionless units
of Sec \ref{subsec:Introduction-of-dimensionless}: the timestep $\Delta t$
is automatically divided by the time scale $t_{0}=\mu/G$.
