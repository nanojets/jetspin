\section{Multiple Timestep Algorithm for Coulomb forces\label{sec:Multiple-Timestep-Algorithm}}

For simulations involving a large number of beads in the calculation
of the long-range Coulomb interactions JETSPIN offers the possibility
of using a multiple timestep algorithm which improves the efficiency.
The method is based on that described by Streett \textit{et al} in
Ref \cite{streett1978multiple} and modified for the special context
of electrospinning process.

The algorithm is activated by \texttt{multiple step yes}. A primary
cutoff $r_{pcut}$ for the Coulomb pair interactions is given by
\texttt{primary cutoff} (mandatory, warning 74). The cutoff $r_{pcut}$
is used to define a standard Verlet neighbor list \cite{allen1989computer},
without a skin: the pairs of beads closer than $r_{pcut}$ when the list
is updated stay in the list until the next update, and the other pairs
stay outside it even if they come closer. Long-range Coulomb forces
derived from beads in the neighbor list
are usually larger than those due to remaining (far) beads not contained
in the list. Therefore, it is possible to assess the Coulomb forces
if the forces derived from the beads in the neighbor list are calculated
at every force evaluation, while those from the remaining beads (outer
forces) are calculated much less frequently, and are merely extrapolated
over the interval. JETSPIN handles this procedure as follows. The Verlet
neighbor list is updated at regular intervals of $n_{mult}$ timesteps,
set by \texttt{multiple step every} $n_{mult}$ (mandatory, warning 76;
$n_{mult}$ must be greater than 2, warning 75). The interval between
updates should be usually of the order of \ensuremath{\sim}50 timesteps.
Whenever the Verlet neighbor list is updated, the Coulomb forces are
computed by the complete direct summation, and the outer forces are
stored. The complete summation is used again at the following force
evaluations, up to the first one at a different time, which gives the
outer forces at a second time. From then on, the Coulomb force acting
on a bead is assessed as sum of the forces derived from the beads in
the neighbor list, calculated explicitly, and of the outer forces,
calculated by linear extrapolation of the two complete summations:
their derivative is the difference of the two outer forces divided
by the time between them, and it is centred at the midpoint of the two
times. The integrators of Sec \ref{sec:Integration-schemes} pass to
every stage of a timestep the time at the beginning of the step: the
complete summation is then used at all the stages of the timestep of
the update and at the first stage of the next one, and the extrapolated
outer forces are constant within a timestep. Only the Kelvin\textendash Voigt
integrators with evaporation pass the time of each stage, so that their
second complete summation is the first stage at a later time. The scheduled
update, at every timestep multiple of $n_{mult}$, waits until the
derivative of the previous update has been taken. Every direct summation
used by the algorithm is complete, in both the one and three dimensional
models: the primary cutoff only selects the pairs of the neighbor list.
With solvent evaporation all the summations use the instantaneous
masses of the beads (Eq \ref{eq:evap-massratio}). It is readily apparent
how this scheme can lead to a significant saving in execution time.

Note that the accuracy of the algorithm is a function of the multiple
step interval, and decreases as $n_{mult}$ increases. Further, the
neighbor list removes the need to scan over all the beads in the simulation
at every timestep. It is worth to highlight that the algorithm is
not time reversible.

The neighbor list is also updated, at the next Coulomb force evaluation,
after every insertion, removal and remeshing of the jet, after a bead
is frozen at the collector (whether it is removed or not), when the
arrays of the jet grow, and at the start of a run, also of a restarted
one. We remark that it is also possible to define in input file a maximum
displacement $\Delta r_{pcut}$ (\texttt{maximum displ}, optional) in
order to achieve a better control on the accuracy of the multiple timestep
algorithm. The maximum displacement $\Delta r_{pcut}$ works as follows:
whenever at least two beads have traveled for a distance larger than
$\Delta r_{pcut}/2$ from their positions at the last update of the
list, the Verlet neighbor list is updated without waiting for the
scheduled update.
The test is made at every Coulomb force evaluation, and every bead
is tested, on every rank, so that serial and parallel runs take the
same decision. Without \texttt{maximum displ} no displacement test
is made.

When \texttt{erms} is printed (Tab \ref{tab:outputlist}), once per
interval, at the last timestep before a scheduled update, JETSPIN also
computes the complete direct summation and compares the extrapolated
outer forces with the exact ones; \texttt{erms} is the mean deviation
per bead, averaged over the intervals checked since the previous print. The
check does not change the forces of that evaluation, which are computed
as at every other evaluation of the interval, so that printing \texttt{erms}
does not change the trajectory.

The time covered by the extrapolation, $n_{mult}\,\Delta t$, must stay
short compared with the time in which the beads move by a significant
fraction of the cutoff, since the outer forces are extrapolated linearly
over the whole interval and the list has no skin; \texttt{maximum displ}
bounds this motion independently of $n_{mult}$. In versions up to 1.22
the second direct summation of the one dimensional model was truncated
at the cutoff, while the summation at the update of the list was not:
the extrapolated far field had the wrong sign and grew over the interval.
This defect, not the choice of the parameters, made a one dimensional
variant of Test Case 2 with an update every 100 steps unstable. Without
the multiple timestep algorithm the primary cutoff truncates only the
direct summation of the one dimensional model (Sec \ref{sec:Input-file}),
which changes the physics of the jet.

With the corrections of version 2.0 (NVFORTRAN 24.3 and GFortran builds),
the one dimensional variant of Test Case 2 with an update every 100 steps,
a cutoff of 1 cm and a maximum displacement of 0.1 cm follows the direct
summation within $1.4\times10^{-7}$ in the tip position over $2\times10^{6}$
steps, with the same 130 insertions, and two MPI ranks reproduce the serial
run exactly; with version 1.22 the same input left the tip at about 1.5 cm
while the direct summation had carried it to 26 cm after 700,000 steps,
and stopped on the numerical instability check at step 1,344,736. Test
Cases 5 and 6 (cutoff 1 cm, update every 100 steps) follow the direct
summation within $6\times10^{-8}$ and $4.4\times10^{-6}$ over $10^{6}$
steps, with the same events. The extrapolation of the outer forces is the
only approximation: Test Case 8 with evaporation and the algorithm switched
on, with a cutoff longer than the jet, gives the rows of the direct
summation exactly over 300,000 steps. With a cutoff of 1 cm, about 1,470
beads and a jet whipping towards the collector, the same run keeps 1.1\%
fewer active beads and makes 146 removals instead of 129 with an update
every 100 steps, 0.1\% fewer and 131 removals with an update every 3 steps,
and 1,990 removals without \texttt{maximum displ}: such a jet needs a short
interval or a larger cutoff.

In the OpenACC build a run with the multiple timestep algorithm executes
the code of the CPU build, since the device step does not support it
(Sec \ref{sec:Running-GPU}); only the complete direct summations of
a jet without evaporation can be offloaded to the GPU.
